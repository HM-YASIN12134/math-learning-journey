# Step 1: Numbers and the Number Line

## Lesson 1: From Counting to the Number Line

### Let's start from zero...

You are counting mangoes in a basket. **1, 2, 3, 4, 5...** While doing this work of counting, the first mathematics that humans discovered is called **number**.

> **Connection Back (Step 0):** In Step 0 you learned — definition means we ourselves fix the meaning of a word. Now with that definition let's ask the first question: **"What is a number, really?"**

---

## 1. Natural Numbers — Counting Numbers

**The problem it was born from:** "How many?" — this question has to be answered.

**Definition (natural numbers):** The numbers with which we count things — 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, ... — these are called natural numbers.

(Somewhere, somewhere 0 is also included in the group of natural numbers. We will talk about this later.)

**These numbers have two beautiful properties:**

**Property 1 — Closure under addition:**  
When two natural numbers are added, the result is also a natural number.  
2 + 3 = 5 ✅ — 5 is also natural.

**Property 2 — Closure under multiplication:**  
When two natural numbers are multiplied, the result is also natural.  
2 × 3 = 6 ✅

**But...**

3 − 5 = ?

Here we got stuck. In the world of natural numbers, there is no answer to this question. Because natural numbers are **not closed under subtraction**.

Division also has a problem: 3 ÷ 2 = ?

---

> **Connection Ahead (Hint — Step 5, Algebra):**  
> Just remember this word "closure" for now. During algebra we will see — "In which world are we playing?" Knowing that is very necessary. Because **if you go to solve an equation without knowing the limits of the world you are playing in, you will make a mistake.**  
> Example: x + 5 = 3 — if the world is natural numbers, there is no answer. If the world is integers, the answer is −2.

---

## 2. The Birth of Zero — "Nothing" Existing

**The problem it was born from:**  
You have 3 mangoes in your basket. You ate all 3. How many are left?

Answer: **Nothing.** But "nothing" has to be written with a number.

The ancients first thought "nothing" means no number at all. But later it was understood — **"nothing" is also a number**, and it has to be written: **0**.

This is a huge discovery of India (Brahmagupta, around 628 CE). Because using 0 as a number means accepting that **"zero" and "absent" are not the same thing.**

**Properties of zero:**

| Rule | Example |
| --- | --- |
| a + 0 = a | 7 + 0 = 7 |
| a − 0 = a | 7 − 0 = 7 |
| a × 0 = 0 | 7 × 0 = 0 |
| a ÷ 0 = undefined | (details on this will come later) |

> **Connection Back (Step 0):**  
> **"a + 0 = a"** — does this seem familiar? Yes! In Step 0 this was an **axiom**. Now you understand why this is an axiom — because it is fundamental, there is nothing more to prove.

> **Connection Ahead (Hint — Step 19, Limits):**  
> The mystery of **a ÷ 0** will stretch all the way to the limits of calculus. When we say "limits of the form 0/0", then the meaning of this zero will become clear.

---

## 3. The Birth of Negative Numbers — "The Opposite Direction"

**The problem it was born from:**  
Outside the temperature is −5°C. What does that mean? It means 5 degrees **below** zero.  
You have 200 taka, you owe 300 taka. That means your position is 100 taka **below** 0.

This concept of "below zero" gave birth to **negative numbers**.

**Definition (integers):** Natural numbers, 0, and the negatives of natural numbers — all together are integers.  
...−3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3...

**Now the subtraction problem is solved:**

3 − 5 = −2 ✅ (in the world of integers this is valid)

**Notice a beautiful thing — pairs:**

| Number | Its pair (opposite) | Sum |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | −3 | 0 |
| 7 | −7 | 0 |
| 100 | −100 | 0 |

Every number has an "opposite", adding which gives **0**. This pair is called **opposite numbers**.

> **Connection Ahead (Hint — Step 5, Algebra):**  
> This idea "adding the opposite number gives 0" will be the **main technique of solving equations** in algebra.  
> x + 5 = 12 → 5 has to be removed from both sides → because 5 and −5 added give 0.

**And zero is a special character here:**
- 0 is neither positive nor negative.
- 0 is the boundary — here the right side is positive, the left side is negative.

---

## 4. The Number Line — One Home for All Numbers

So far we got three types of numbers: natural, 0, negative.  
But how will we **see them together?**

Answer: **Number Line.**

**How to draw:**

1. Draw a straight line.
2. Take a point in the middle, name it **0**.
3. Take another point at a fixed distance to the right, name it **1**. This distance is the **unit distance**.
4. Keep going right at the same distance: 2, 3, 4, ...
5. Keep going left at the same distance: −1, −2, −3, ...

```
←──|──|──|──|──|──|──|──|──→
   -3  -2  -1   0   1   2   3
```

**Now see the magic:**

Now **every number has a fixed home** on this line. And—

> **Going right the number is bigger, going left the number is smaller.**

This one sentence is one of the most powerful sentences in all of mathematics. Because:

| Comparison | Meaning on the number line |
| --- | --- |
| 5 > 2 | 5 is to the right of 2 |
| −3 < 1 | −3 is to the left of 1 |
| −5 < −2 | −5 is to the left of −2 (even though 5 > 2!) |

**Careful!** With negative numbers it seems reversed: −5 is actually **smaller** than −2, because −5 is further left.

> **Connection Ahead (Hint — Step 5, Inequalities):**  
> When in algebra we write "x > 3", then it will mean — **on the number line x will sit to the right of 3.**  
> Solving an inequality means marking a part of the number line. Without this number line, understanding inequalities is impossible.

> **Connection Ahead (Hint — Step 7, Coordinate Plane):**  
> Now you saw one number line. **What happens when two number lines are placed perpendicularly is the coordinate plane (x-y plane)!**  
> That is, the whole stage for drawing graphs stands on this number line. When we draw graphs of functions, it is just an extension of this one idea.

> **Connection Ahead (Hint — Step 19, Limits):**  
> "The number is going right-left" — this idea will later become "the value of x is going towards 2". The motion of the number line → limits of calculus.

---
## 5. Addition-Subtraction on the Number Line (A Little Bridge)

With the number line, addition-subtraction can be seen with the eyes:

| Work | Rule | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Addition | Go right | 2 + 3 = 5 (from 0 to 2, then 3 to the right) |
| Subtraction | Go left | 5 − 3 = 2 |
| Adding negative | Go left | 3 + (−5) = −2 |
| Subtracting negative | Go right | 3 − (−5) = 8 |

**Notice the last line:** Subtracting a negative means **going right**.  
3 − (−5) = 3 + 5 = 8

> This will later become a golden rule of algebra: **"Subtraction means adding the opposite"**.

---

## 6. Absolute Value — Distance from Zero

**Definition (absolute value):** The absolute value of a number means — how far the number is from 0 (ignoring direction).

|−5| = 5 (5 units away from 0)  
|5| = 5 (5 units away from 0)  
|0| = 0

**Interesting thing:** −5 and 5 — both are 5 units away from 0! Only the direction is different.

> **Connection Ahead (Hint — Step 5, Absolute Value Equations):**  
> |x| = 5 means x = 5 or x = −5. Two answers!  
> This "two answers" idea will come again and again in algebra.

# Step 1, Lesson 2: Fractions and Rational Numbers

## 1. The Problem from Which Fractions Were Born

On the number line, so far we have only placed integers:

```
←──|──|──|──|──|──|──|──|──→
   -3  -2  -1   0   1   2   3
```

But here there are many **empty spaces**!  
What is between 0 and 1? Nothing? Or is there something?

**Problem:**  
You have 1 pizza. If divided equally among 2 friends, how much will each get?

Answer: **Half.**  
But how will we write this with a number?

We cannot write it with an integer. Because the answer is between 0 and 1.  
The solution to this problem gave birth to **fractions**.

---

## 2. What is a Fraction?

**Definition (fraction):**  
A fraction is a number of the form $\frac{a}{b}$, where:

- $a$ and $b$ are integers
- $b \neq 0$
- $a$ = numerator
- $b$ = denominator

**Meaning:**  
$\frac{a}{b}$ means — if a thing is divided into $b$ equal parts, then $a$ parts.

**Examples:**

| Fraction | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $\frac{1}{2}$ | Dividing a thing into 2 parts, 1 part |
| $\frac{3}{4}$ | Dividing a thing into 4 parts, 3 parts |
| $\frac{5}{2}$ | 5 halves = 2.5 |

> **Connection Back (Step 1, Lesson 1):**  
> Now we can place fractions on the number line. $\frac{1}{2}$ will sit exactly halfway between 0 and 1.  
> That is, the empty spaces of the number line are being filled.

---

## 3. Fractions on the Number Line

```
←──|──|──|──|──|──|──|──|──→
  -1      -1/2   0   1/2   1
```

$\frac{1}{2}$ means half the way to the right from 0.  
$-\frac{1}{2}$ means half the way to the left from 0.

Now the number line is filled more.

---

## 4. Equivalent Fractions — Same Thing, Different Appearance

Are $\frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{2}{4}$ different?

No! Both are half.

```
1/2  =  2/4  =  3/6  =  4/8 ...
```

**Why?**  
Because $\frac{2}{4}$ means 2 divided by 4.  
$\frac{2}{4} = \frac{2 \div 2}{4 \div 2} = \frac{1}{2}$.

**Rule:**  
If the numerator and denominator are multiplied or divided by the same number, the value of the fraction does not change.

> **Connection Ahead (Hint — Step 5, Algebra):**  
> This very rule will come again and again in algebra during **fraction simplification** and **equation solving**.  
> For example: $\frac{x}{2} = \frac{3}{4}$ — here the concept of equivalent fractions will be needed to make both sides equal.

---

## 5. Rational Numbers — The Big Family of Fractions

**Definition (rational number):**  
A number that can be written in the form $\frac{p}{q}$, where $p, q$ are integers and $q \neq 0$, is called a **rational number**.

**Notice:**  
All integers are also rational!  
Because $5 = \frac{5}{1}$, $-3 = \frac{-3}{1}$, $0 = \frac{0}{1}$.

Therefore:

```
Natural numbers ⊂ Integers ⊂ Rational numbers
```

> **Connection Back (Step 0):**  
> Here I used the symbol **⊂** — it means "subset".  
> In Step 2 you learned sets. Now see, the world of numbers is also each a set.

---

## 6. Density of Rational Numbers — No Empty Spaces!

**Question:** Is there any rational number between $\frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{3}{4}$?

**Answer:** Yes! Take their average:

$$
\frac{\frac{1}{2} + \frac{3}{4}}{2} = \frac{\frac{2}{4} + \frac{3}{4}}{2} = \frac{\frac{5}{4}}{2} = \frac{5}{8}
$$

$\frac{5}{8}$ is between $\frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{3}{4}$.

In this way, no matter how small an interval you take, more rational numbers can be found within it.  
This is called the **density of rational numbers**.

Yet the number line is still not completely filled! Because there are some numbers that are not rational — such as $\sqrt{2}$, $\pi$. Those are **irrational numbers**. That is the next lesson.

---

## 7. Addition-Subtraction-Multiplication-Division of Fractions

### Addition-Subtraction:
Denominators have to be made equal.

$$
\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{3}{6} + \frac{2}{6} = \frac{5}{6}
$$

### Multiplication:
Numerator × numerator, denominator × denominator.

$$
\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{4}{5} = \frac{8}{15}
$$

### Division:
Multiply the first by the reciprocal of the second.

$$
\frac{2}{3} \div \frac{4}{5} = \frac{2}{3} \times \frac{5}{4} = \frac{10}{12} = \frac{5}{6}
$$

> **Connection Ahead (Hint — Step 5, Algebra):**  
> $\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c}$ — this rule will be useful in algebraic fraction equations.

---

## 8. Decimal Form — Another Face of Rational Numbers

Every rational number can be written as a decimal. There are two types:

| Type | Example | Characteristic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Terminating decimal | $\frac{1}{2} = 0.5$ | Comes to an end |
| Repeating decimal | $\frac{1}{3} = 0.333...$ | A pattern returns again and again |

**Important:**  
The decimal form of a rational number is either **terminating** or **repeating**.  
A decimal that neither terminates nor repeats is **irrational**. (Next lesson)

# Step 1, Lesson 3: Irrational Numbers and Real Numbers

## 1. The Problem from Which Irrational Numbers Were Born

**Pythagoras' crisis:**

Think, you have a square piece of land, each of whose sides has length **1 unit**.

```
+-----+
|     |
|     | 1
|     |
+-----+
  1
```

Now if you draw a straight line from one corner to the opposite corner (the diagonal). Question: **What is the length of the diagonal?**

From Pythagoras' theorem we know:
$$
1^2 + 1^2 = c^2
$$
$$
2 = c^2
$$
$$
c = \sqrt{2}
$$

Now the question: **Can $\sqrt{2}$ be written in the form $\frac{p}{q}$?**

Pythagoras' followers first thought all numbers are rational. But when they tried to write $\sqrt{2}$ as a fraction, they got stuck. Finally they **proved** that this is impossible.

This discovery was a huge shock in the history of mathematics. Because at that time people thought the number line meant only a thing filled with fractions. $\sqrt{2}$ showed that on the number line there are **other types of numbers** too.

> **Connection Back (Step 0):**  
> Here we will use **proof**. In Step 0 you learned — "Let, then show with reasoning." Now using that structure of proof we will show that $\sqrt{2}$ is not rational.

---

## 2. Proof: $\sqrt{2}$ is Irrational

This is a **proof by contradiction**.

**Let,** $\sqrt{2}$ be rational. Then according to definition:
$$
\sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q}
$$
where $p, q$ are integers, $q \neq 0$, and $\frac{p}{q}$ is in simplest form (that is, there is no common factor between $p$ and $q$).

Squaring both sides:
$$
2 = \frac{p^2}{q^2}
$$
$$
p^2 = 2q^2
$$

**Step 1:** $p^2 = 2q^2$ means $p^2$ is even.  
If $p^2$ is even, then $p$ must also be even (because the square of an odd number is odd).

Then $p = 2k$ (for some integer $k$).

**Step 2:** Substitute $p = 2k$:
$$
(2k)^2 = 2q^2
$$
$$
4k^2 = 2q^2
$$
$$
2k^2 = q^2
$$

Now $q^2 = 2k^2$ means $q^2$ is even.  
Then $q$ is also even.

We got $p$ even and $q$ even.  
But at the beginning we assumed $\frac{p}{q}$ is in simplest form — that is, there is no common factor between $p$ and $q$.  
Now **both** are divisible by 2! That is, **both** are even!

**This is a contradiction.**

**Conclusion:** Our assumption $\sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q}$ was wrong.  
Therefore, $\sqrt{2}$ is not rational. $\sqrt{2}$ is **irrational**. ∎

> **This is wonderful, isn't it?** The "if-then" you learned in Step 0 just became useful. This proof is one of the most beautiful proofs in mathematics.

## 3. Irrational Numbers — Definition and Examples

**Definition (irrational number):**  
A number that cannot be written in the form $\frac{p}{q}$ (where \(p, q\) are integers, $q \neq 0$) is called an **irrational number**.

**Examples:**

| Number | Why it is irrational |
| --- | --- |
| $\sqrt{2}$ | The proof above |
| $\sqrt{3}$ | A similar proof |
| $\pi$ | Can be proved (Lindemann, 1882) |
| $e$ | Can be proved (Euler) |

**How to recognize in decimal:**

- The decimal of a rational number is either **terminating** (such as \(0.5\)) or **repeating** (such as \(0.333...\)).
- The decimal of an irrational number is **non-terminating and non-repeating** (such as $\sqrt{2}$ = 1.41421356...\), there is no pattern).

---

## 4. Real Numbers — The Ocean of All Numbers

**What we have found so far:**

```
Natural numbers (1, 2, 3, ...)
    ↓
Integers (..., −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, ...)
    ↓
Rational numbers (can be written in fraction form)
    ↓
Irrational numbers (cannot be written in fraction form)
```

**Definition (real number):**  
Rational and irrational — all numbers together are **real numbers**.

$$
\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{Q} \cup \mathbb{I}
$$

> **Connection Back (Step 2):**  
> Here I used the symbol **∪** — it means **Union**.  
> In Step 2 you learned sets. Now see — the world of numbers can also be written in the language of sets!

**Important point:**  
Now the number line is **completely filled**. There is no empty space.  
For every point on the number line there is a real number, and for every real number there is a point.

This is called the **Completeness of Real Numbers**.

> **Connection Ahead (Hint — Step 19, Limits):**  
> This concept of "completeness" is the foundation of calculus. The limit works because there is no empty space on the number line. If there were empty spaces, the concept of limit would collapse.

---

## 5. The Family Tree of the World of Numbers

```
                    Real Numbers (ℝ)
                   /              \
           Rational (ℚ)          Irrational (𝕀)
          /       \                |
   Integers (ℤ)   Fractions      √2, √3, π, e
        |
   Natural (ℕ)
```

**Notice:**
- $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}$
- $\mathbb{I} \subset \mathbb{R}$
- $\mathbb{Q} \cap \mathbb{I} = \emptyset$ (rational and irrational are separate groups, no number is in both groups)

## 6. Placing Irrational Numbers on the Number Line

How to place $\sqrt{2}$ on the number line?  
Draw a square whose side is 1. Its diagonal's length is $\sqrt{2}$.  
Measure the diagonal's length with a compass and place it to the right from 0.

```
←──|──|──|──|──|──|──|──→
   0  1  √2  2  3
```

In this way any $\sqrt{n}$ can be placed.
